# Tao Te Ching — Annotated Edition

**Laozi (traditional attribution)**

### The Way Chapters

#### 1

The Way that can be spoken
is not the true Way;
the name that can be named
is not the true name.
Without a name: the beginning of Heaven and Earth.
With a name: the mother of all things.
So, ever without desire,
see its mystery;
ever with desire,
see its boundary.
These two arise together
and differ in name;
together they are called mystery.
Mystery within mystery—
the gate of all wonders.

**Comments:**

A name gives attention a place to begin. Experience keeps unfolding beyond the boundaries that a word draws. “Common sense relativizes, strives for objectivity, corrects itself” ([PhS §1.1 ¶17](../philosophers-stone/book-en.md)) offers a useful discipline: let understanding grow beyond its first formulation.

#### 2

When all beneath Heaven know beauty as beauty,
ugliness is already there.
When all know good as good,
what is not good is already there.
So being and nonbeing arise from one another;
difficult and easy complete one another;
long and short set one another's measure;
high and low lean toward one another;
tones and sounds harmonize with one another;
before and after follow one another.
Therefore the sage acts through non-doing
and carries out a teaching without words.
All things arise, and he does not turn them away;
he gives them life but does not possess them,
acts but does not rely on the deed,
brings the work to completion but does not claim it.
Because he does not claim it,
it does not leave him.

**Comments:**

Beauty and goodness become easier to recognize when we notice the contrasts that give them shape. A quiet room, an open doorway, and the space inside a cup each show how what is present and absent work together. Cooperative action can leave room for others to complete what no single person can do alone.

#### 3

Do not exalt the worthy,
and the people will not contend.
Do not prize goods hard to obtain,
and the people will not become thieves.
Do not display what stirs desire,
and the people's hearts will not be thrown into disorder.
Thus in the sage's rule:
empty their hearts,
fill their bellies,
weaken their ambitions,
strengthen their bones.
Always keep the people without knowledge and without desire.
Make those with knowledge dare not act.
Act through non-doing,
and nothing will remain ungoverned.

**Comments:**

Read inwardly, the ruler becomes our capacity to direct attention, and the crowd becomes the impulses competing for it. Inner order grows when these impulses cooperate around a freely understood purpose. “Unity must be based on freedom.” ([PhS §1.21 ¶1](../philosophers-stone/book-en.md)) Self-discipline can follow the same principle, cultivating cooperation among our capacities.

#### 4

The Way is hollow;
use it, and it may never fill.
Deep—perhaps the forebear of all things.
It blunts their sharpness,
unties their knots,
softens their light,
mingles with their dust.
So deep—perhaps it is there.
I do not know whose child it is;
it seems to come before the Lord.

**Comments:**

A hollow vessel can serve because it has room to receive and be used. The image invites a practical question: where might less certainty leave more space for a new response? What is useful here is not emptiness as an object, but the possibility of making room.

#### 5

Heaven and Earth are not benevolent:
they treat all things as straw dogs.
The sage is not benevolent:
he treats the people as straw dogs.
Is not the space between Heaven and Earth like a bellows?
Hollow, yet it does not give way;
move it, and ever more comes forth.
Too much talk soon exhausts.
Better to hold to the center.

**Comments:**

The straw-dog image is deliberately stark: the poem does not promise that the world will arrange itself around our comfort. The bellows offers a different picture, with an open center that keeps giving when moved. Between those images, the advice to hold the center suggests steadiness without excess speech.

#### 6

The spirit of the valley does not die:
this is called the mysterious female.
The gate of the mysterious female—
this is called the root of Heaven and Earth.
Endlessly, as if it were there;
use it without toil.

**Comments:**

The valley and gate make generative power feel like a place one can enter rather than a force one can command. The image stays suggestive: it offers a root and an opening, while leaving their source unnamed. In experience, quiet receptivity can sometimes renew what effort alone cannot sustain.

#### 7

Heaven lasts; Earth endures.
Heaven and Earth can last so long:
They do not live for themselves;
therefore they live long.

So the sage puts himself last,
and comes first;
leaves himself out of account,
and is kept safe.
Is it not because he is without self-interest
that he can fulfil his own good?

**Comments:**

Heaven and Earth last by not living for themselves; the sage’s self-placement follows that image. Putting oneself last can make room for others, though the closing question keeps a personal benefit in view. The passage holds both gestures together instead of reducing them to a simple rule of self-denial.

#### 8

The highest good is like water.
Water is good at helping all things;
it does not contend.
It settles where people disdain to be,
so it comes near the Way.

In dwelling, be good as the ground.
In heart, be good as the deep.
In giving, be good in kindness.
In speech, be good in trust.
In setting things right, be good at governing.
In work, be good at what you can do.
In movement, be good in timing.
Because it does not contend,
there is no blame.

**Comments:**

Water helps without competing for the highest place. Its example reaches into ordinary choices: where we settle, how we speak, and when we act. A useful practice is to contribute fully without needing every contribution to become a contest.

#### 9

Hold it, fill it to the brim—
better to stop.
Hone it to a point—
it cannot stay sharp for long.
Gold and jade fill the hall:
no one can keep them safe.
Grow proud of wealth and rank,
and you bring trouble on yourself.
When the work is done, withdraw.
This is Heaven's Way.

**Comments:**

A vessel filled to the brim has no room for another useful addition; a sharpened edge cannot stay sharp forever. The chapter carries that limit from objects to wealth and rank. Knowing when to stop can protect the value of work already done.

#### 10

Hold the body-soul as one:
can you keep them from parting?
Gather breath; make it supple:
can you be a newborn?
Wash clean the dark seeing:
can you leave it without a flaw?
Love the realm; govern the people:
can you do it without knowing?
As Heaven's gate opens and closes,
can you be the female?
With clear sight reaching everywhere,
can you refrain from action?
Give life; nurture.
Give life, yet do not possess;
act, yet do not rely;
lead, yet do not rule.
This is hidden potency.

**Comments:**

The questions join bodily steadiness, supple breath, care, and action that does not dominate. They make discipline sound less like tightening control and more like keeping several capacities in balance. The final pattern—give life, nurture, and do not possess—offers a concrete test for care.

#### 11

Thirty spokes share one hub;
where it is empty, the cart can roll.
Knead clay to make a vessel;
where it is empty, the vessel can hold.
Cut doors and windows to make a room;
where they are empty, the room can be lived in.
What is there brings advantage;
what is not there makes it work.

**Comments:**

A hub, a vessel, and a room all depend on space that is not occupied. Their usefulness is visible in what they let happen: a cart rolls, a vessel holds, a room welcomes life. When planning, it can help to ask what must remain open for a design to work.

#### 12

The five colours blind the eye.
The five tones deafen the ear.
The five flavours dull the mouth.
Racing and hunting drive the heart wild.
Rare goods hinder a person's way.
So the sage tends the belly, not the eye:
lets go of that
and takes this.

**Comments:**

Bright colours, loud tones, rich tastes, racing, and rare goods crowd the senses until attention is pulled in every direction. The turn toward the belly rather than the eye is a reminder to notice what sustains us, not only what dazzles. Choosing less stimulation can make room for a steadier appetite.

#### 13

Favour and disgrace—both startle.
Great trouble is prized as the body.
What is meant by “favour and disgrace—both startle”?
Favour puts one below:
gain it, and start;
lose it, and start.
That is what “favour and disgrace—both startle” means.
What is meant by “great trouble is prized as the body”?
I have great trouble
because I have a body.
Were I without a body,
what trouble could reach me?
So, one who prizes the body as all under Heaven
may be entrusted with all under Heaven;
one who loves the body as all under Heaven
may be given all under Heaven to care for.

**Comments:**

Favour can unsettle as much as disgrace because both place our worth in someone else’s hands. The body is described as a source of vulnerability, yet the closing image also connects care for the body with care for the realm. A trusted responsibility may ask for humility without asking us to deny our embodied life.

#### 14

Look for it: unseen.
Name it “indistinct.”
Listen for it: unheard.
Name it “inaudible.”
Reach for it: beyond grasp.
Name it “subtle.”
These three cannot be pursued to the end;
mingling, they become one.
Above, it is not bright;
below, it is not dark.
Unceasing, it cannot be named;
it returns to no-thing.
This is called the form without form,
the image of no-thing:
call it elusive, dim.
Meet it: its head is not seen.
Follow it: its rear is not seen.
Hold to the Way of old
to govern what is here now.
Know the beginning of old—
this is the Way's thread.

**Comments:**

The unseen, unheard, and unreachable are not captured by adding more labels. The poem follows them into a single elusive presence, then turns toward the practical task of meeting what is here. Sometimes attention is clearest when it can stay with what it cannot fully grasp.

#### 15

Those skilled in the Way of old
were fine, mysterious, far-reaching,
so deep they could not be known.
Because they could not be known,
I can only sketch their bearing:
cautious—as one crossing a stream in winter;
wary—as if fearing neighbours on every side;
solemn in their bearing;
loose, like ice about to melt;
plain, like uncarved wood;
open, like a valley;
murky, like troubled water.
Who can let the murky grow still
and slowly clear?
Who, at rest, can move for long
and slowly come to life?
Those who keep this Way do not seek fullness.
Not being full,
they can be worn and not made new.

**Comments:**

The old practitioners are sketched through careful, fluid images rather than a fixed definition. Troubled water becomes clear by being allowed to settle; a person at rest can move again in time. Patience here is active: it waits for a response to become possible.

#### 16

Reach the utmost emptiness;
keep to stillness, deep and firm.
All things rise together;
I watch their return.
Teeming, each thing
returns again to its root.
Return to the root: stillness.
This is return to one's lot.
Return to one's lot: the enduring.
Know the enduring: clarity.
Not knowing what endures,
one acts rashly—and disaster follows.
Knowing what endures, one is receptive;
receptive, one becomes impartial;
impartial, one becomes whole;
whole, one is of Heaven;
Heaven is the Way;
the Way endures, long-lasting.
To the end of life: no danger.

**Comments:**

The chapter follows many things rising and returning to a root, then links steadiness with clear action. When we recognize a pattern that keeps recurring, a pause can help us respond rather than act rashly. Stillness is presented as a return that changes how we meet the next moment.

#### 17

Of the highest ruler,
the people know only that he is there.
The next they draw near to, and praise;
the next they fear;
the next they scorn.
When trust is not enough,
mistrust follows.
How sparing he is with words!
Work done, the task fulfilled,
the people all say:
“We are so of ourselves.”

**Comments:**

The least praised ruler is known mainly by the work completed and the people’s sense that they acted for themselves. The passage makes trust visible through how little the ruler needs to announce. In shared work, leadership can sometimes be measured by whether others retain a real sense of their own agency.

#### 18

When the great Way falls away,
benevolence and righteousness arise.
When wisdom and cleverness emerge,
great deceit appears.
When the six family bonds fall out of harmony,
filial care and parental love arise.
When the state lies in darkness and disorder,
loyal ministers appear.

**Comments:**

The poem notices that named virtues often become prominent when a larger harmony has already weakened. Its sequence is a warning about symptoms, not a reason to dismiss kindness or family care. When a practice needs constant display, it may be worth asking what ordinary trust has lost.

#### 19

Cut off holiness; cast out cleverness,
and the people gain a hundredfold.
Cut off benevolence; cast out righteousness,
and the people return to filial and parental care.
Cut off craft; cast out profit,
and thieves and robbers disappear.
These three, as adornments, do not suffice;
so give them something to hold to:
see the plain, embrace uncarved wood;
make less of self, desire less.

**Comments:**

The chapter’s commands to cast out familiar virtues are sharp, and its promised social effects should not be softened. Its closing images offer a different practical direction: plainness, less self-importance, and fewer wants. A simpler life may make room for care without turning care into a public badge.

#### 20

Leave learning behind, and worry falls away.
A reverent “yes” and a careless “ah”—how far apart?
Good and bad—how far apart?
What people fear, one must fear.
How vast it is, without end!
Everyone is buoyant, as at the great sacrifice,
as if climbing a terrace in spring.
I alone am still, before any sign,
like a newborn who has not yet smiled;
worn thin, with nowhere to call home.
Everyone has more than enough;
I alone seem to have lost all.
Mine is a fool's heart—clouded, unformed.
Others shine clear;
I alone am dim.
Others see sharply;
I alone am dull.
I drift like the sea,
unmoored, with no stopping place.
Everyone has a purpose;
I alone am stubborn, lowly, out of place.
I alone differ from other people,
and prize being fed by the Mother.

**Comments:**

The speaker stands apart from the crowd’s certainty and describes a mind that feels cloudy, unformed, and without a settled home. That discomfort is not dressed up as easy superiority; it remains a vulnerable voice. The final image of being fed by the Mother suggests dependence as a source of nourishment.

#### 21

The bearing of great potency
follows only the Way.
The Way, as a thing,
is dim, elusive.
Elusive, dim; within it there is an image.
Dim, elusive; within it there is a thing.
Deep, dark; within it there is an essence.
That essence is wholly real; within it is trust.
From now back to ancient times,
its name has never fallen away;
by it we survey the beginnings of all things.
How do I know what those beginnings are like?
By this.

**Comments:**

The chapter moves from dim, elusive images to the claim that an essence is real and trustworthy, then admits that its beginnings are known only “by this.” It offers a felt basis for understanding without turning that basis into a method anyone can simply copy. A good account can begin with what experience actually shows.

#### 22

When bent, one is made whole;
when crooked, one becomes straight;
when hollow, one is filled;
when worn, one is renewed;
with little, one gains;
with much, one is bewildered.
So the sage holds to the One,
the pattern for all under Heaven.
Not showing himself, he is clear;
not declaring himself right, he is seen;
not boasting, he has merit;
not exalting himself, he endures.
Because he does not contend,
no one under Heaven can contend with him.
The old saying, “Bent, and whole”—
how could it be empty words?
Whole, truly, all return to it.

**Comments:**

The opening reversals show how bending, yielding, or having little can create room to become whole. The sage’s later refusal to boast follows the same pattern: less self-display can make work more visible. When a familiar position feels incomplete, changing angle may reveal what it can hold.

#### 23

Speaking little comes of itself.
Thus a gale does not last all morning;
a cloudburst does not last all day.
Who brings these about?
Heaven and Earth.
Even Heaven and Earth cannot make them last—
how much less can people?
Those who work with the Way are one with the Way;
those with potency are one with potency;
those who are lost are one with loss.
The Way gladly welcomes those who are one with it;
potency gladly welcomes those who are one with potency;
loss gladly welcomes those who are one with loss.
When trust runs short,
mistrust follows.

**Comments:**

A gale and a cloudburst do not last all day; intense conditions have limits even when we cannot command their arrival. The chapter turns that observation toward speech, conduct, and trust. Responding in proportion to a moment can be wiser than trying to make every mood permanent.

#### 24

On tiptoe, one cannot stand firm;
with a great stride, one cannot walk far.
Who shows himself is not clear;
who declares himself right is not made manifest;
who boasts has no merit;
who exalts himself does not endure.
As for the Way, these are
leftover food, needless growths.
Things may loathe them;
one who has the Way does not abide in them.

**Comments:**

Standing on tiptoe and taking an oversized stride both promise more than they can sustain. The later examples connect self-display with work that does not last. A measured pace may carry us farther than trying to appear already at the destination.

#### 25

A thing takes shape in the undivided,
born before Heaven and Earth.
Silent, empty,
it stands alone and does not change;
circling everywhere, it is never spent.
It may be the mother of all under Heaven.
I do not know its name.
I call it the Way.
Forced to name it, I call it Great.
Great: it passes on.
Passing on: it reaches far.
Reaching far: it returns.
So the Way is great,
Heaven is great,
Earth is great,
and the king is great too.
Within the realm there are four greats,
and the king is one of them.
People take Earth as their model;
Earth takes Heaven as its model;
Heaven takes the Way as its model;
the Way takes what is so of itself as its model.

**Comments:**

The unnamed thing is described through movement—passing on, reaching far, and returning—rather than a settled title. The king appears among four greats, but the sequence then places people, Earth, and Heaven in relations of modeling. The image invites attention to how each level depends on something beyond itself.

#### 26

Weight is the root of lightness;
stillness, the ruler of unrest.
So the sage travels all day
without leaving the baggage behind.
Though grand sights abound,
he rests at ease, beyond their pull.
How could the lord of ten thousand chariots
take his own person lightly before the realm?
Lightness loses the root;
unrest loses the ruler.

**Comments:**

Weight anchors lightness, and stillness anchors unrest. The sage’s baggage keeps the image practical: even amid grand sights, the journey does not abandon what must be carried. In a demanding role, steadiness begins with knowing what deserves to remain in view.

#### 27

Skilled at walking, leaving no tracks;
skilled at speech, leaving no flaw;
skilled at counting, needing no tallies.
Skilled at closing: no bolt,
yet it cannot be opened.
Skilled at tying: no cord,
yet it cannot be undone.
So the sage is always skilled at saving people:
no one is cast aside.
Always skilled at saving things:
nothing is cast aside.
This is called inheriting the light.
The good person teaches the one who is not good;
the one who is not good is the good person's resource.
If the teacher is not honored,
if the resource is not cherished,
even the wise fall into great delusion.
This is the heart of the mystery.

**Comments:**

The skilled walker, speaker, and keeper leave no trace of forceful effort; the sage’s skill then appears in refusing to cast people or things aside. The teacher and learner are reciprocal: each relationship can reveal something the other lacks. Help is strongest when it preserves a place for everyone to participate.

#### 28

Know the male,
keep to the female:
be the world's ravine.
As the world's ravine,
enduring potency does not leave;
return to the newborn.

Know the white,
keep to the black:
be the world's pattern.
As the world's pattern,
enduring potency does not stray;
return to the boundless.

Know glory,
keep to disgrace:
be the world's valley.
As the world's valley,
enduring potency comes to fullness;
return to uncarved wood.

When uncarved wood is scattered,
it becomes vessels.
The sage uses them
as officers and chiefs.
Thus the great ordering does not cut apart.

**Comments:**

The chapter repeatedly asks us to know one pole and keep to its counterpart: male and female, white and black, glory and disgrace. Its valley and uncarved-wood images make room for what is not polished or dominant. Holding a capacity without being ruled by its opposite can make it more useful.

#### 29

When one wants to take the realm
and act upon it,
I see that it cannot be done.
The realm is a sacred vessel:
it cannot be acted upon.
Whoever acts upon it ruins it;
whoever grasps it loses it.

Some things go ahead, others follow;
some breathe out softly, others blow;
some are strong, others frail;
some are checked, others ruined.
Therefore the sage removes the extreme,
removes extravagance,
removes excess.

**Comments:**

The realm is called a sacred vessel that is damaged by grasping or acting upon it. The following contrasts refuse a single rigid pattern, and the sage removes extremes rather than imposing one shape. In shared life, care may begin by noticing where intervention would break what it means to help.

#### 30

One who helps a ruler by the Way
does not use arms to strengthen the realm.
Such deeds tend to return.
Where an army has camped,
thorns and brambles grow.
After a great army,
bad years are sure to come.

The skilled reach the result, and no more;
they dare not use it to seize strength.
Bring the result, but do not boast;
bring the result, but do not claim merit;
bring the result, but do not grow proud.
Reach the result only when there is no choice;
reach it, but do not press your strength.

When things are strong, they grow old:
this is called being contrary to the Way.
What is contrary to the Way soon ends.

**Comments:**

The chapter links armies with thorns and bad years, then warns against turning a result into pride or further force. It keeps the cost of military action in view instead of celebrating victory as proof of virtue. When force seems unavoidable, restraint can limit what it sets in motion.

#### 31

Fine weapons are ill-omened tools;
things may loathe them.
So the one who follows the Way
does not abide among them.

At home, the noble honors the left;
in war, the right.
Arms are ill-omened tools,
not the noble's tools.
When their use cannot be avoided,
calm restraint is best.

Victory is not beautiful;
to call it beautiful
is to delight in killing.
Whoever delights in killing
cannot prevail in the world.

For auspicious rites, the left is honored;
for inauspicious rites, the right.
The deputy general stands on the left;
the supreme general on the right—
this is to treat them by funeral rites.

When many have been killed,
mourn and weep for them.
Victory in battle, too,
is treated by funeral rites.

**Comments:**

Weapons are called ill-omened even when their use cannot be avoided; victory is treated with mourning rather than celebration. The passage’s funeral imagery asks us to remember those killed, not only the outcome claimed by a side. A victory that requires grief deserves sober reflection.

#### 32

The Way is nameless, ever.
Though the uncarved block is small,
all beneath Heaven cannot make it subject.

If lords and kings could keep to it,
the myriad things would come as guests of their own accord.
Heaven and Earth join
and send down sweet dew;
no one commands the people—
they find their own even balance.

With the first making, names arise.
Once names have arisen,
one must know where to stop.
Knowing when to stop is how danger is escaped.

As the Way stands in the world,
so streams and valleys stand
in relation to rivers and seas.

**Comments:**

The uncarved block is small, yet cannot be made subject; once names arise, the poem says to know where to stop. Rivers and valleys offer a scale image for how the low receives the flow of many streams. Simplicity here is not emptiness of life, but freedom from needless control.

#### 33

Knowing others is wisdom;
knowing oneself is illumination.
Overcoming others takes strength;
overcoming oneself is true strength.
Who knows contentment is rich.
Who persists has resolve.
Who keeps their place endures.
Who dies yet is not lost
has long life.

**Comments:**

Knowing oneself and overcoming oneself are called forms of clarity and strength, distinct from defeating others. Contentment, persistence, and keeping one’s place then describe a durable kind of richness. Strength can be quiet: it need not depend on winning against someone else.

#### 34

The great Way flows wide—
it reaches left and right.
All things rely on it and live;
it turns none away.
Its work is done,
yet it claims no possession.
It clothes and feeds all things,
yet is no one's master.
Ever without desire,
it may be called small.
All things return to it,
yet it is no one's master;
it may be called great.
Because it never makes itself great,
it can accomplish greatness.

**Comments:**

The great Way reaches left and right, sustains all things, and claims no possession for its work. The poem holds wide reach beside smallness: something need not make itself important to accomplish much. Care can be generous without turning the recipient into property.

#### 35

Hold fast to the great image:
all beneath Heaven comes to it.
Coming, they meet no harm;
they rest in peace, ease, and fullness.

Music and rich fare
make the passing traveler pause.
The Way, as it leaves the mouth,
is bland, without taste.
Look: there is too little to see.
Listen: there is too little to hear.
Use it: it cannot be exhausted.

**Comments:**

Music and rich food can hold a traveler briefly, while the Way’s taste seems almost too slight to notice. Its usefulness appears through continued use rather than spectacle. Some supports are easiest to recognize by what remains possible after the excitement passes.

#### 36

Would you draw it in?
First, stretch it out.
Would you weaken it?
First, make it strong.
Would you cast it down?
First, raise it up.
Would you take from it?
First, give to it.
This is called subtle illumination.

The soft and weak overcome
the hard and strong.
Keep fish in the deep pool;
the state's keen instruments
must not be shown to the people.

**Comments:**

The repeated reversals show power growing through a change in position: stretch before drawing in, raise before casting down. The soft and weak can overcome the hard and strong, while the state’s sharp instruments are kept from display. Timing and discretion matter as much as force.

#### 37

The Way always acts without forcing,
yet nothing is left undone.
If lords and kings could keep to it,
all things would change of themselves.
If, in that change, desire stirred,
I would still it with nameless uncarved wood.
Nameless uncarved wood—
they too would then be without desire.
Without desire, through stillness,
all under Heaven would settle of itself.

**Comments:**

The chapter’s opening imagines action without forcing, but it also names rulers and the quieting of desire; that tension remains part of the passage. Its closing picture is of people settling as stillness takes hold. Inwardly, we can read this as a hope that less compulsion allows competing impulses to settle.

### The Potency Chapters

#### 38

The highest De does not display De;
therefore it has De.
The lower De never lets go of De;
therefore it has no De.
The highest De acts without forcing, with no aim in acting.
The lower De acts, with an aim in acting.
The highest benevolence acts, with no aim in acting.
The highest righteousness acts, with an aim in acting.
The highest ritual acts; when no one answers,
it bares its arms and drags them into line.

Thus, when the Way is lost, De appears;
when De is lost, benevolence appears;
when benevolence is lost, righteousness appears;
when righteousness is lost, ritual appears.
Ritual is loyalty and trust grown thin,
the beginning of disorder.
Foreknowledge is the flower of the Way,
and the beginning of folly.

So the great person stays with what is thick,
not what is thin;
with what is real,
not its flower.
Thus they leave that and take this.

**Comments:**

德 [potency] shows in the quality of action before it is proclaimed. When understanding weakens, people may cling to forms and enforce the appearance of virtue. “love cannot be commanded” ([PhS §1.9 ¶8](../philosophers-stone/book-en.md)) brings the question back to what gives a practice life: willing participation and genuine care.

#### 39

Of old, those who attained the One were these:
Heaven attained the One and thereby became clear;
Earth attained the One and thereby became settled;
spirits attained the One and thereby became numinous;
valleys attained the One and thereby became full;
the myriad things attained the One and thereby came to life;
lords and kings attained the One
and thereby became the measure of all under Heaven.

Consider what follows:
Were Heaven without clarity, it would fear splitting;
were Earth without settledness, it would fear upheaval;
were spirits without numinous power, they would fear ceasing;
were valleys without fullness, they would fear running dry;
were the myriad things without life, they would fear extinction;
were lords and kings without nobility and height,
they would fear being overthrown.

Thus the honored takes the lowly as its root;
the high takes the low as its foundation.
This is why lords and kings call themselves
孤 [orphan], 寡 [widowed], and 不穀 [unworthy].
Is this not taking the lowly as their root?
Is it not?

Count the parts of a carriage:
no carriage remains.
Do not seek to be polished like jade;
be rugged like stone.

**Comments:**

Heaven, Earth, valleys, and living things each depend on attaining the One; the honored ruler is also reminded of lowliness. The carriage parts show the same point in a concrete way: naming every piece does not leave a carriage. A whole is more than a title or a polished fragment.

#### 40

Return is the Way's movement.
Weakness is the Way's use.

All things beneath Heaven
come to birth from what is;
what is comes to birth from what is not.

**Comments:**

The chapter gives the Way a movement of return and a use in weakness. It then links what exists with what does not, without explaining either as a final substance. In ordinary life, retreat or a pause can sometimes be the movement that makes a fresh beginning possible.

#### 41

The best learners, hearing the Way,
strive to live it.
The middling learners, hearing the Way,
seem to keep it, seem to lose it.
The poorest learners, hearing the Way,
laugh out loud at it.
Unless it is laughed at,
it is not enough to be called the Way.

So the old sayings go:
The bright Way seems dim.
The forward Way seems to retreat.
The level Way seems rough.
The highest potency seems a valley.
The greatest whiteness seems stained.
Broad potency seems not enough.
Upright potency seems furtive.
True substance seems to change.
The greatest square has no corners.
The great vessel is completed late.
The great sound is scarcely heard.
The great image has no shape.
The Way is hidden, without a name.
Only the Way
knows how to lend and bring to completion.

**Comments:**

The old sayings make the Way sound dim, rough, or unfinished where expectation wants brightness and polish. The contrasts are not defects to fix; they ask us to reconsider how we recognize value. A quiet or late-forming work may carry more than it first reveals.

#### 42

The Way gives birth to One;
One gives birth to Two;
Two gives birth to Three;
Three gives birth to all things.
All things bear yin on their backs
and embrace yang;
the mingled breaths make harmony.

What people dislike,
these names alone: “orphan,” “widowed,” “unworthy”;
yet lords and princes take them as titles.
Thus some things lose, and thereby gain;
others gain, and thereby lose.

What people teach, I teach too:
the forceful and violent do not die a natural death.
I will make this the father of my teaching.

**Comments:**

The poem traces a chain from One through Two and Three to all things, then describes yin and yang meeting in mingled breath. The names “orphan,” “widowed,” and “unworthy” are disliked but adopted by rulers, turning lowly titles into a source of perspective.

#### 43

The softest beneath Heaven
runs through the hardest beneath Heaven.
What has no substance enters where there is no gap.
From this I know the benefit of non-action.
Teaching without words,
the benefit of non-action—
few beneath Heaven attain them.

**Comments:**

The softest passes through hard places, and what has no substance can enter where there is no gap. These images make non-action practical: influence need not always arrive as pressure. A teaching can also work through what it allows another person to see for themselves.

#### 44

Name and person—which is dearer?
Person and possessions—which are worth more?
Gain and loss—which does greater harm?

Therefore, greatly cherishing
is sure to bring great expense;
hoarding much
is sure to bring heavy loss.
Know contentment: no disgrace.
Know when to stop: no danger.
Thus you can last a long time.

**Comments:**

The questions about name, person, possessions, gain, and loss move quickly to the costs of cherishing and hoarding. Contentment and knowing when to stop are presented as ways to avoid needless harm. The passage invites a simple check: what matters more than the thing we are trying to keep?

#### 45

Great completion seems wanting;
its use is not worn away.
Great fullness seems empty;
its use is never exhausted.
Great straightness seems bent;
its use does not hold its place.
Great skill seems clumsy;
its use does not cease.
Great eloquence seems halting;
its use does not fail.

Restlessness overcomes cold;
stillness overcomes heat.
Clear stillness is the world's true measure.

**Comments:**

The poem pairs great completion with apparent lack, great fullness with emptiness, and great eloquence with hesitation. The value of each quality is measured by continued use, not by an impressive surface. Clear stillness can be a better measure than a display of perfection.

#### 46

When the Way holds sway beneath Heaven,
war-horses are put to hauling manure.
When the Way is absent beneath Heaven,
war-horses are born on the outskirts.
No calamity is greater than not knowing contentment;
no fault greater than wanting to gain.
The sufficiency of knowing sufficiency
is always enough.

**Comments:**

When the Way prevails, war-horses haul manure; when it does not, they are born at the frontier. The contrast turns political order into a visible difference in what animals are made to do. The chapter places contentment beside that cost: wanting more can carry a whole community toward war.

#### 47

Without going out the door,
one knows the world;
without peering through the window,
one sees Heaven's Way.
The farther one goes,
the less one knows.
Therefore the sage
knows without going,
names without seeing,
completes without doing.

**Comments:**

The chapter contrasts knowledge without travel with a warning that going farther can bring less understanding. It favors a kind of attention that can notice a pattern without collecting every possible view. This is not a call to avoid the world, but a reminder that distance and information do not automatically produce insight.

#### 48

In learning, one adds day by day;
in the Way, one loses day by day.
Lose, and lose again,
until non-action is reached.
By non-action, nothing is left undone.
The realm is won always through non-interference;
once one interferes,
one is not fit to win the realm.

**Comments:**

Learning adds, while following the Way lets go, again and again, until nothing remains undone through non-action. The political lines make the cost of interference explicit. In practice, removing an unnecessary step can sometimes make a task more complete, not less.

#### 49

The sage has no fixed heart;
the people's hearts become his heart.
To the good, I am good;
to those not good, I am also good:
Such is the power of goodness.
Those worthy of trust, I trust;
those unworthy of trust, I also trust:
Such is the power of trust.
The sage, among all under Heaven,
draws close, draws close;
for all under Heaven he mingles their hearts.
The people all strain their ears and eyes;
the sage makes them all children.

**Comments:**

The sage’s heart changes with the people’s hearts, and the passage pairs trust with those who seem unworthy of it. That response is presented as a power of goodness and trust, not as a tidy rule that every situation will reward. Listening closely can begin by meeting a person before fixing them in a category.

#### 50

Coming out into life, entering death.
Those who live are three in ten;
those who die are three in ten;
people who, in living, move toward death's ground
are also three in ten.
Why is this so?
Because they make too much of living.
I have heard that one skilled at tending life,
walking on land, does not meet rhinoceros or tiger;
entering an army, is not struck by armor or weapons.
The rhinoceros finds nowhere to thrust its horn;
the tiger nowhere to set its claws;
the weapon nowhere to lodge its blade.
Why is this so?
Because there is no ground of death for them.

**Comments:**

The opening counts life and death in stark proportions, then offers a striking image of moving safely through danger. Its closing explanation is that there is no ground of death for those people; the poem leaves the image’s force in tension with the hazards it names. It invites attention to how we inhabit life without pretending that ordinary risk disappears.

#### 51

The Way gives them life;
De nourishes them;
things give them form;
circumstance brings them to completion.
Thus among all things
none fails to honor the Way
or prize De.
The Way is honored,
De is prized,
without anyone commanding it;
it is always so of itself.
So the Way gives them life;
De nourishes them.
They are grown and nurtured,
settled and matured,
sheltered and covered.
Giving life without possessing,
acting without relying on the deed,
leading without ruling.
This is dark De.

**Comments:**

Life, nourishment, form, and circumstance each contribute to what a thing becomes. None is commanded to honor the Way or prize 德 [potency]; the sequence presents this as occurring of itself. The final refrain offers a pattern of giving and leading without possession or rule.

#### 52

All under Heaven has a beginning;
it is taken as the mother of all under Heaven.
Once you have found the mother,
you know her children.
Once you know her children,
return and keep to their mother:
to the end of your life, no danger.
Block its openings;
close its gates:
to the end of your life, no toil.
Open its openings;
busy yourself with its affairs:
to the end of your life, no rescue.
Seeing the small is clarity;
keeping to the soft is strength.
Use its light,
return to its brightness;
bring no harm upon yourself.
This is practicing the constant.

**Comments:**

The mother and children images connect a beginning with what grows from it; the chapter then contrasts closing openings with opening them. Seeing the small and keeping to the soft are called clarity and strength. Attention to an early sign can prevent a small disturbance from becoming the whole story.

#### 53

If I had even a little knowing,
walking the great Way,
the only thing I would fear is straying.
The great Way is very level,
yet people love side paths.
The court is swept clean,
the fields lie thick with weeds,
the granaries are very empty;
while they wear patterned silk,
carry sharp swords,
eat and drink their fill,
and have wealth to spare.
This is robbers' boasting.
It is not the Way!

**Comments:**

If I had even a little knowing, walking the great Way, I would fear straying while the court is polished, the fields overgrown, and the granaries empty. The contrast exposes a public cost hidden beneath display and surplus. The chapter’s final rejection of robbers’ boasting makes its criticism direct.

#### 54

What is well planted is not uprooted;
what is well held is not taken away.
Sons and grandsons will not cease
to offer the ancestral rites.
Cultivated in the person,
its De becomes real;
cultivated in the family,
its De overflows;
cultivated in the village,
its De grows long;
cultivated in the state,
its De becomes abundant;
cultivated throughout all under Heaven,
its De reaches everywhere.
Thus by the person, observe the person;
by the family, observe the family;
by the village, observe the village;
by the state, observe the state;
by all under Heaven, observe all under Heaven.
How do I know all under Heaven is so?
By this.

**Comments:**

What is well planted lasts, and the chapter follows 德 [potency] from a person outward through family, village, state, and all under Heaven. Each is observed through its own person, family, village, state, or all under Heaven. The pattern suggests that public life is not separate from the habits practiced at home.

#### 55

Rich in De,
like a newborn:
bees, scorpions, snakes do not sting it;
beasts do not seize it,
birds of prey do not strike.

Its bones are weak, its sinews soft,
yet its grip is firm.
Before it knows the joining of female and male,
yet its member rises:
essence at its height.

It cries all day and is not hoarse:
harmony at its height.
To know harmony is to know what endures;
to know what endures is clarity.
To add to life is an ill omen.

The heart driving the breath is force.
When things grow strong, they grow old:
this is not the Way.
What is not the Way soon ends.

**Comments:**

The newborn’s firm grip gives a simple image of strength within softness. Gentleness leaves room to respond, recover, and adapt. Inner discipline can develop through attention to rhythm and balance, allowing capacities to mature through use ([PhS §1.17 ¶2](../philosophers-stone/book-en.md)). This gives practical meaning to caring for the body as an instrument of experience.

#### 56

One who knows does not speak;
one who speaks does not know.

Close the openings,
shut the gates;
blunt the sharpness,
untangle its divisions;
soften its light,
join it with the dust.
This is called mysterious union.

So none can draw near,
none can keep distant;
none can profit it,
none can harm it;
none can honor it,
none can disgrace it.
Therefore it is honored by all under Heaven.

**Comments:**

The chapter pairs knowing with silence and speaking with not knowing, then turns to closing, softening, and joining with dust. Its final exclusions—no drawing near or keeping distant, no profit or harm—describe a life that resists easy social ranking. Sometimes understanding is shown by the care with which we choose not to claim the last word.

#### 57

Rule the state by the straight;
use the unorthodox in war;
take the realm without meddling.

How do I know this is so?
By this:
the more taboos under Heaven,
the poorer the people;
the more sharp tools the people have,
the darker the state grows;
the more skill and cunning among people,
the more strange things arise;
the more laws and commands are displayed,
the more thieves and robbers appear.

So the sage says:
“I do not act,
and the people change of themselves.
I love stillness,
and the people set themselves right.
I do not meddle,
and the people grow rich of themselves.
I have no desire,
and the people become plain, like uncarved wood, of themselves.”

**Comments:**

The ruler says, “I do not meddle,” and the people change and prosper of themselves, yet the chapter also gives direct counsel about statecraft and war. Read inwardly, its list of prohibitions and sharp tools can prompt us to notice how excessive self-control breeds inner resistance.

#### 58

When its rule is dull and unintrusive,
its people are plain and whole.
When its rule is sharp and watchful,
its people are wanting and worn.

Good fortune leans on misfortune;
misfortune hides within good fortune.
Who knows where it ends?
There is no fixed norm.

The straight turns strange;
the good turns uncanny.
How long people have been lost!

So the sage is square, but does not cut;
has an edge, but does not wound;
is straight, but does not overreach;
is bright, but does not dazzle.

**Comments:**

A dull, unintrusive rule is paired with people who are plain and whole; a sharp, watchful rule with people who are worn. Fortune and misfortune then lean into one another, with no fixed point where the change must end. The sage’s clear edge without injury offers a practical image of firmness without overreach.

#### 59

In governing people and serving Heaven,
nothing is better than restraint.

Only through restraint
does one prepare early.
Preparing early is called
heaping up De.
With De heaped high,
Nothing is left unconquered.
When nothing is left unconquered,
none knows its limit.
When none knows its limit,
one can hold the kingdom.

Holding the kingdom's mother,
one can endure for long.
This is called deep roots,
a firm rootstock:
the Way of long life
and long sight.

**Comments:**

Restraint makes early preparation possible, and the chapter links preparation with accumulated 德 [potency]. Its images of a kingdom’s mother, deep roots, and a firm rootstock all point toward durability built over time. A small steady practice may support more than a last-minute display of effort.

#### 60

Governing a great state
is like cooking a small fish.

Bring the realm under the Way,
and its ghosts do not wield their spirit-power.
Not that their spirit-power is absent;
their power does not harm people.
It is not that their power does no harm;
the sage, too, does not harm people.

When neither harms the other,
their De joins and returns there.

**Comments:**

Governing a great state is compared to cooking a small fish: too much handling can spoil what is delicate. The passage extends this restraint to spirits and people, carefully distinguishing their power from the harm it might do. Good care can require a light touch while still taking responsibility.

#### 61

A great state is the low reach,
where all under Heaven meet;
the female of all under Heaven.
The female always conquers the male by stillness;
by stillness, she takes the lower place.

So, when a great state lies below a small state,
it takes the small state;
when a small state lies below a great state,
it takes the great state.
One lies low and takes;
one lies low and is taken.

A great state wants no more
than to gather and care for people;
a small state wants no more
than to enter service to people.
When both get what each wants,
the greater ought to lie low.

**Comments:**

A great state is pictured as a low reach where others meet, and the political sequence turns on who lies below and who is taken in. Both large and small states want a relationship, though their aims differ; the greater is told to lie low. The image makes humility a condition of influence, not a denial of power.

#### 62

The Way is the hidden shelter of all things,
the treasure of the good,
the safeguard of those who are not good.

Fine words can be traded;
honorable conduct can raise a person above others.
When people are not good,
why cast them away?

So, at the enthronement of the Son of Heaven
and the appointment of the Three Ministers,
though a jade disk, clasped in both hands,
goes before a team of four horses,
that is not as good as sitting and offering this Way.

Why did people of old prize this Way?
Was it not said:
seek, and you will obtain;
be guilty, and be let off?
That is why it is prized under Heaven.

**Comments:**

The Way is called shelter and treasure for good and not-good alike, and the passage asks why anyone should be cast away. Even the ceremonial gifts for a new ruler are judged less valuable than sitting and offering the Way. The strongest gift may be a place where a person can begin again.

#### 63

Act without forcing;
tend to affairs through non-doing;
taste what has no taste.

Make the small great,
the few many.
Answer resentment with virtue.

Plan for the hard while it is easy;
do the great while it is small.
The hard affairs under Heaven
must begin in the easy;
the great affairs under Heaven
must begin in the small.

Thus the sage never undertakes great things,
and so can accomplish them.

A light promise earns little trust;
to treat everything as easy brings much difficulty.
Thus the sage still treats things as difficult,
and in the end has no difficulty

**Comments:**

The chapter brings large affairs back to small beginnings and hard tasks back to the moment when they are still easy. The sage’s refusal to treat anything as effortless helps avoid a false promise that difficulty will vanish. Careful attention at the start can save force later.

#### 64

What is at rest is easy to hold;
what has not yet shown a sign is easy to plan for.
What is brittle is easy to break apart;
what is minute is easy to scatter.

Act on what is not yet there;
set things right before disorder.

An embrace-wide tree grows from a tiny shoot;
a nine-storied tower rises from a heap of earth;
a journey of a thousand miles begins beneath the feet.

Those who act ruin it;
those who grasp lose it.
Thus the sage does not act,
and so does not fail;
does not grasp,
and so does not lose.

In their affairs, people often fail
when they are near completion.
Care for the end as for the beginning,
and no affair will fail.

Thus the sage desires what is not desired,
does not prize goods hard to get;
learns what is not learned,
and returns to what the many have passed by.

He aids all things in being so of themselves,
and does not dare to act

**Comments:**

A tiny shoot, a heap of earth, and the ground beneath the feet make beginnings tangible. The chapter warns that people often fail near completion, then asks us to care for the ending as carefully as the start. A task is not finished just because its hardest visible part is behind us.

#### 65

Those of old who were good at following the Way
did not use it to enlighten the people,
but to make them ignorant.

The people are hard to govern
because they have too much cleverness.
To govern a state by cleverness
is the state's bane;
not to govern a state by cleverness
is the state's blessing.

Knowing these two is also a rule.
To know the rule always
is called deep de.

Deep de—how deep,
how far!
It runs counter to all things;
then great harmony is reached.

**Comments:**

The chapter bluntly says earlier rulers used the Way to keep people ignorant, and calls clever government the state’s bane. The later image of running counter to things before reaching harmony invites reflection on how cleverness may obscure a simpler truth.

#### 66

Rivers and seas can be kings of the hundred valleys
because they are good at lying below them;
that is how they can be kings of the hundred valleys.

So, if you would stand above the people,
you must speak to them from below.
If you would lead the people,
you must put yourself behind them.

Thus the sage stands above,
and the people do not feel his weight;
he stands in front,
and the people are not harmed.

So all under Heaven gladly press him forward
and are never weary of him.
Because he does not contend,
no one under Heaven can contend with him.

**Comments:**

Rivers and seas become rulers of valleys by lying below them; the sage’s leadership follows the same image. The text promises that people do not feel his weight or suffer harm, a demanding standard for anyone who leads. Influence may be welcomed when it makes room rather than pressing down.

#### 67

All under heaven call my Way great,
and unlike anything else.
Just because it is great,
it is unlike.
If it were like anything,
how small it would have grown by now.

I hold three treasures,
and keep them close:
the first is compassion,
the second, thrift,
the third, not daring to be first under heaven.

Compassion makes me brave.
Thrift lets me give freely.
Not daring to be first
makes me fit to lead.

Now to cast off compassion and reach for bravery,
to cast off thrift and reach for abundance,
to cast off the rear and take the lead—
that is death.

With compassion, one can win in battle;
with compassion, one can stand firm in defense.
When Heaven is to save someone,
it shields them with compassion.

**Comments:**

Compassion, thrift, and not daring to be first are called treasures, each joined to courage, generosity, or the ability to lead. The poem warns that discarding these qualities for their more forceful opposites leads toward death. Its account of compassion in battle keeps gentleness and protection together.

#### 68

The skilled officer makes no show of force.
The skilled fighter does not flare with anger.
The one skilled at defeating an enemy does not engage them.
The one skilled at employing people places himself beneath them.

This is called the virtue of not contending;
this is called drawing on people's strength;
this is called matching Heaven,
the ancient height of mastery.

**Comments:**

The skilled officer avoids showing force; the skilled fighter does not flare with anger; the skilled leader places himself beneath others. Mastery is described through what it does not need to display. Calm restraint can make strength useful without turning it into intimidation.

#### 69

Those who use arms have a saying:
“I dare not play the host;
I would rather be the guest.
I dare not advance an inch;
I would rather withdraw a foot.”

This is called marching without a march,
baring an arm without an arm,
engaging without an enemy,
holding without a weapon.

No calamity is greater than making light of the enemy.
To make light of the enemy
nearly costs me my treasures.

So, when opposing armies meet,
the ones who grieve are the ones who prevail.

**Comments:**

The military saying prefers being the guest to the host and withdrawal to an eager advance. Its final image says the grieving prevail when armies meet, keeping sorrow inside the account of victory. Caution toward an enemy is not weakness here; it is a way to remember what conflict costs.

#### 70

My words are very easy to understand,
very easy to put into practice.
Yet no one under heaven can understand them;
no one can put them into practice.

My words have an ancestor;
my affairs have a ruler.
Only because they do not know this
do they fail to know me.

Those who know me are few.
Those who follow me are precious.

That is why the sage wears coarse cloth,
with jade held against the chest.

**Comments:**

The speaker says the words are easy to understand and practice, yet almost no one does either. The hidden ancestor and ruler suggest that familiar language may still rest on something not readily recognized. Coarse cloth with jade against the chest gives a final image of value kept close rather than displayed.

#### 71

To know that you do not know:
that is best.
Not to know, yet claim to know:
that is a sickness.

Only when one is sick of sickness
is one free of sickness.

The sage is free of sickness
because he is sick of sickness;
that is why he is free of it.

**Comments:**

Knowing that we do not know is called the best beginning; claiming knowledge without it is named a sickness. The repeated image of becoming sick of sickness makes correction part of recovery. “assumption is preliminary, valid only until one has come to know” ([KofR §1.2 ¶11](../knowledge-of-reality/book-en.md)) offers a related reminder to revise what we think we know.

#### 72

When the people no longer fear authority,
a greater awe is upon them.

Do not press in on their homes;
do not weigh down their lives.
Only by not weighing them down
will they not grow weary of you.

That is why the sage knows himself
but does not put himself on display;
loves himself
but does not exalt himself.

So he lets that go
and takes up this.

**Comments:**

When authority is no longer feared, a greater awe follows; the ruler is then warned not to press into people’s homes or weigh down their lives. The final distinction between knowing and displaying oneself offers a quiet measure of self-respect. Care for the self need not become self-promotion.

#### 73

Brave in daring, one is killed;
brave in not daring, one lives.
These two may bring benefit or harm.
What Heaven hates—who knows why?
For this the sage hesitates.

The Way of Heaven does not contend,
yet excels at victory;
does not speak,
yet excels at answering;
is not summoned,
yet comes of itself;
unhurried, it excels at planning.
Heaven’s net is vast and wide:
sparse, yet nothing slips through.

**Comments:**

The chapter refuses to make bravery simple: daring may kill, while not daring may preserve life, and either can bring benefit or harm. Heaven’s Way is described through effective answers without contention or speech. The vast net catches everything while remaining sparse, an image of reach without constant pressure.

#### 74

If the people do not fear death,
why threaten them with death?
If the people always feared death,
and those who deviate
I could seize and kill,
who would dare?

There is always an executioner who kills.
To take the executioner's place and kill
is to take the great carpenter's place
and hew.
Few who take the great carpenter's place
escape without injuring their own hands.

**Comments:**

The passage asks whether threats can govern people who do not fear death, then compares taking the executioner’s place to handling a great carpenter’s tool. Its warning is physical and political: the person who takes over killing may injure their own hands. Authority does not make violence harmless.

#### 75

The people go hungry
because those above consume too much tax revenue;
that is why they are hungry.
The people are hard to govern
because their superiors act too much;
that is why they are hard to govern.
The people make light of death
because their superiors seek life in excess;
that is why they make light of death.

The one who does not act for life's sake
is better than the one who prizes life.

**Comments:**

The people’s hunger and difficulty are tied directly to those above consuming too much and acting too much. The chapter does not blame the people for conditions created by their rulers. Its closing contrast asks whether life is best served by making survival itself an object of excess pursuit.

#### 76

Alive, a person is soft and weak;
dead, hard and strong.
All things, grasses and trees,
alive are tender and fragile;
dead, withered and dry.

The hard and strong belong to death;
the soft and weak belong to life.
When an army is strong, it does not win;
when wood grows strong, it meets the weapon.
The great and strong are placed below;
the soft and weak are placed above.

**Comments:**

A living person and a living tree are soft and fragile; in death they become hard and dry. The poem makes softness a sign of life, then carries the contrast into armies and rank. Adaptability is not merely yielding—it is a capacity that rigid strength can lose.

#### 77

Is not the Way of Heaven
like drawing a bow?
What is high, it presses down;
what is low, it raises up.
What has excess, it reduces;
what lacks, it fills.

The Way of Heaven
reduces what has excess
and fills what is lacking.
The way of people is not so:
they take from what is lacking
to serve what has excess.

Who can offer their excess
to all beneath Heaven?
Only one who holds the Way.
Thus the sage acts, yet does not rely on it;
when the work is done, the sage does not rest on its merit;
does not wish to display worth.

**Comments:**

The bow lowers what is high and raises what is low; the chapter contrasts this balancing image with people taking from the poor to serve the rich. The sage’s question is who can offer their excess to everyone. “to give, help, serve” ([PhS §3.30 ¶5](../philosophers-stone/book-en.md)) provides a useful phrase for the direction of that offering.

#### 78

Nothing beneath Heaven is softer or weaker than water;
yet in attacking what is hard and strong,
nothing can surpass it.
Nothing can take its place.

The weak overcome the strong;
the soft overcome the hard.
Everyone beneath Heaven knows this;
no one can put it into practice.

Therefore the sage says:
“To bear the state's filth
is to be lord of its altars.
To bear the state's ill omen
is to be king beneath Heaven.”

Straight words sound like their opposite.

**Comments:**

Water is soft, yet it can overcome what is hard; the poem says everyone knows this, but no one can put it into practice. The next lines make leadership a willingness to bear what others reject. A difficult truth may sound opposite to expectation because it asks us to carry responsibility, not claim status.

#### 79

Settle a great resentment,
and resentment remains.
How could this be good?

So the sage holds the left tally
and makes no demands of others.
The one with de keeps the tally;
the one without de exacts the tax.

Heaven's Way takes no sides:
it is always with the good.

**Comments:**

Settling a great resentment does not guarantee that resentment is gone. The sage holds one side of the tally without demanding payment from others, while the chapter contrasts that conduct with exacting the debt. Reconciliation may begin with an honest account of what remains unresolved.

#### 80

Let the country be small, its people few.
Let there be tools tenfold, a hundredfold,
yet let none be used.
Let the people take death seriously
and not move far away.

Though they have boats and carts,
let there be no occasion to ride in them.
Though they have armor and weapons,
let there be no occasion to display them.
Let people return to knotted cords and use them.

Let them find their food sweet,
their clothes beautiful,
their homes peaceful,
their customs a joy.

Neighboring states within sight of one another,
the sounds of dogs and cocks heard across;
the people, grown old and dying,
never visit one another.

**Comments:**

The small country and its few people are imagined with useful tools that remain unused, peaceful homes, and customs people enjoy. The neighboring states can hear one another but do not visit; the picture values sufficiency and distance together. It is a social ideal, not a claim that the outside world has disappeared.

#### 81

Faithful words are not beautiful;
beautiful words are not faithful.
The good do not argue;
those who argue are not good.
Those who know are not widely learned;
the widely learned do not know.

The sage does not hoard.
The more he does for others,
the more he has.
The more he gives to others,
the more he has.

Heaven's Way benefits and does no harm.
The sage's Way acts without contending.

**Comments:**

Generosity exercises the capacities it expresses: attention, understanding, and willingness to help. What circulates can enrich a shared life. The direction is simple: “to give, help, serve” ([PhS §3.30 ¶5](../philosophers-stone/book-en.md)). Giving becomes a practice of unity, through which both the recipient and the giver can grow.
